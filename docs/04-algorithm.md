# 4. The Algorithm and its Correctness

This section presents the search the engine performs, proves it sound and
complete, and analyses its cost. The presentation is in pseudocode. The two
TypeScript realisations are discussed in
[Section 5](./05-implementation.md).

## 4.1 The algorithm

The search assigns courses one at a time in a fixed order, maintaining a running
mask of the slots already claimed. A candidate section is accepted only when it
claims no slot already taken, and rejection prunes the whole subtree beneath it.

$$
\begin{array}{l}
\textbf{procedure } \mathrm{Solve}(C, S, \mu, \Gamma, k) \\
\quad \text{order } C \text{ as } c_1, \dots, c_d \text{ by ascending } b_{c} \\
\quad \textbf{for each } c \in C \textbf{ do order } S(c) \text{ by ascending } |\mu(s)| \\
\quad R \leftarrow \emptyset \\
\quad \mathrm{Extend}(1,\ \emptyset,\ \langle\rangle) \\
\quad \textbf{return } R \\[1.2ex]

\textbf{procedure } \mathrm{Extend}(i,\ W,\ A) \\
\quad \textbf{if } |R| \ge k \textbf{ then return} \\
\quad \textbf{if } i > d \textbf{ then } R \leftarrow R \cup \{A\};\ \textbf{return} \\
\quad \textbf{for each } s \in S(c_i) \text{ in order } \textbf{do} \\
\quad\quad \textbf{if } s \in \Gamma \textbf{ then} \\
\quad\quad\quad \mathrm{Extend}(i + 1,\ W,\ A \cdot s) \\
\quad\quad \textbf{else if } W \cap \mu(s) = \emptyset \textbf{ then} \\
\quad\quad\quad \mathrm{Extend}(i + 1,\ W \cup \mu(s),\ A \cdot s) \\
\end{array}
$$

Here $A \cdot s$ denotes the assignment $A$ extended with $s$, and $R$ is the
set of reported selections. The test $W \cap \mu(s) = \emptyset$ is performed as
$\beta(W) \wedge \beta(\mu(s)) = 0$ by Proposition 1.

Two ordering heuristics appear in $\mathrm{Solve}$.

- Courses are tried in increasing order of the number of sections they offer.
  This places the most constrained variable first, so that a dead end is
  discovered as close to the root as possible.
- Sections within a course are tried in increasing order of mask size, that is,
  in increasing order of $|\mu(s)|$, computed as a population count over the
  bitmask. A section occupying fewer slots leaves more room for later courses.

Both are heuristics. Neither is used by any proof below, and correctness does
not depend on them. Their effect on cost is not measured in this document, and
no claim is made about it.

## 4.2 The loop invariant

Fix an instance and the orders chosen by $\mathrm{Solve}$. Write
$\mathrm{real}(A) = \{\, s \in A : s \notin \Gamma \,\}$ for the non-ghost
sections of a partial assignment, as in
[Section 2.2](./02-problem-formulation.md#22-ghost-sections).

> **Invariant $\mathcal{I}(i, W, A)$.** At every call of
> $\mathrm{Extend}(i, W, A)$,
>
> 1. $A$ assigns exactly one section to each of $c_1, \dots, c_{i-1}$ and none
>    to any other course;
> 2. $\displaystyle W \;=\; \bigcup_{s \in \mathrm{real}(A)} \mu(s)$;
> 3. the sections of $\mathrm{real}(A)$ are pairwise disjoint.

> **Lemma 4.** $\mathcal{I}$ holds at every call of $\mathrm{Extend}$.

*Proof.* By induction on the depth of the call.

**Base.** The initial call is $\mathrm{Extend}(1, \emptyset, \langle\rangle)$.
Condition 1 holds because $A$ is empty and the range $c_1, \dots, c_0$ is empty.
Condition 2 holds because the union over the empty set is $\emptyset = W$.
Condition 3 holds vacuously, as $\mathrm{real}(\langle\rangle) = \emptyset$ has
no pairs.

**Step.** Suppose $\mathcal{I}(i, W, A)$ holds and the body makes a recursive
call. The call is generated by some $s \in S(c_i)$ through one of the two
branches, and in both cases the new assignment is $A' = A \cdot s$ with depth
$i + 1$.

Condition 1 for $A'$ is immediate. By hypothesis $A$ covers exactly
$c_1, \dots, c_{i-1}$, and $s \in S(c_i)$ adds exactly $c_i$, so $A'$ covers
exactly $c_1, \dots, c_i$.

For conditions 2 and 3, distinguish the branches.

*Ghost branch,* $s \in \Gamma$. Then $W' = W$ and
$\mathrm{real}(A') = \mathrm{real}(A)$, since $s$ is excluded from
$\mathrm{real}$ by definition. Both conditions carry over unchanged from the
hypothesis.

*Conflict-tested branch,* $s \notin \Gamma$ and $W \cap \mu(s) = \emptyset$.
Then $W' = W \cup \mu(s)$ and
$\mathrm{real}(A') = \mathrm{real}(A) \cup \{s\}$.

Condition 2 follows by computing
$$W' = W \cup \mu(s)
= \Big( \bigcup_{s' \in \mathrm{real}(A)} \mu(s') \Big) \cup \mu(s)
= \bigcup_{s' \in \mathrm{real}(A')} \mu(s') ,$$
where the middle equality uses the induction hypothesis.

Condition 3 requires every pair in $\mathrm{real}(A')$ to be disjoint. Pairs
drawn from $\mathrm{real}(A)$ are disjoint by hypothesis. A pair consisting of
$s$ and some $s' \in \mathrm{real}(A)$ satisfies
$$\mu(s) \cap \mu(s') \subseteq \mu(s) \cap \bigcup_{s'' \in \mathrm{real}(A)} \mu(s'')
= \mu(s) \cap W = \emptyset ,$$
using condition 2 of the hypothesis and then the branch guard. Note that $s$
belongs to $S(c_i)$ while every $s'$ belongs to some $S(c_j)$ with $j < i$, so
$s \neq s'$ and the pair is genuine.

Both branches preserve $\mathcal{I}$, completing the induction. $\blacksquare$

## 4.3 Soundness

> **Theorem 4 (soundness).** Every selection reported in $R$ is a conflict-free
> selection in the sense of SECTION SELECTION WITH GHOSTS.

*Proof.* A selection is added to $R$ only at the line guarded by $i > d$. At
that point Lemma 4 gives $\mathcal{I}(d+1, W, A)$.

Condition 1 with $i = d + 1$ says $A$ assigns exactly one section to each of
$c_1, \dots, c_d$, which is all of $C$. Hence $A$ is a selection.

Condition 3 says the sections of $\mathrm{real}(A)$ are pairwise disjoint. By
the definition in
[Section 2.2](./02-problem-formulation.md#22-ghost-sections), this is exactly
the requirement that for every pair of distinct courses $c, c'$ with
$A(c) \notin \Gamma$ and $A(c') \notin \Gamma$,
$$\mu(A(c)) \cap \mu(A(c')) = \emptyset .$$

Therefore $A$ is conflict-free. $\blacksquare$

Theorem 4 is stated over the ghost problem deliberately. When
$\Gamma \neq \emptyset$ the returned schedules are **not** conflict-free in the
ordinary sense, and a pair of ghost sections may overlap freely. Any claim that
the engine returns non-overlapping timetables is true only for
$\Gamma = \emptyset$.

## 4.4 Completeness

Completeness is stated for the algorithm as written above, with no result bound
and no resource cutoff. Write $\mathrm{Solve}^{\infty}$ for
$\mathrm{Solve}$ with $k = \infty$.

> **Theorem 5 (completeness).** If the instance is satisfiable then
> $\mathrm{Solve}^{\infty}$ reports at least one conflict-free selection.
> Moreover it reports every conflict-free selection.

*Proof.* Let $\sigma$ be any conflict-free selection. For $0 \le i \le d$ let
$\sigma_i$ denote the partial assignment $\langle \sigma(c_1), \dots, \sigma(c_i) \rangle$,
and let
$$W_i \;=\; \bigcup_{\substack{j \le i \\ \sigma(c_j) \notin \Gamma}} \mu(\sigma(c_j)) .$$

**Claim.** For every $i$ with $0 \le i \le d$, the call
$\mathrm{Extend}(i + 1, W_i, \sigma_i)$ occurs.

The claim is proved by induction on $i$.

For $i = 0$ the call is $\mathrm{Extend}(1, \emptyset, \langle\rangle)$, which
$\mathrm{Solve}$ makes unconditionally, and $W_0 = \emptyset$ as the union over
an empty index set.

Assume the call $\mathrm{Extend}(i, W_{i-1}, \sigma_{i-1})$ occurs for some
$1 \le i \le d$. With $k = \infty$ the guard $|R| \ge k$ never fires, and since
$i \le d$ the guard $i > d$ does not fire, so the body runs its loop over
$S(c_i)$. The loop visits every section of $S(c_i)$, and $\sigma(c_i)$ is one of
them. Consider the iteration at $s = \sigma(c_i)$.

If $s \in \Gamma$, the ghost branch is taken with no test, producing the call
$\mathrm{Extend}(i + 1, W_{i-1}, \sigma_{i-1} \cdot s)$. Here
$W_i = W_{i-1}$ because $s$ is a ghost, and
$\sigma_{i-1} \cdot s = \sigma_i$, so the call is
$\mathrm{Extend}(i + 1, W_i, \sigma_i)$ as required.

If $s \notin \Gamma$, the guard $W_{i-1} \cap \mu(s) = \emptyset$ must be shown.
Expanding,
$$W_{i-1} \cap \mu(s)
= \bigcup_{\substack{j < i \\ \sigma(c_j) \notin \Gamma}} \big( \mu(\sigma(c_j)) \cap \mu(s) \big) .$$
Every term is empty. Indeed $j < i$ gives $c_j \neq c_i$, and both
$\sigma(c_j)$ and $s = \sigma(c_i)$ are non-ghost, so conflict-freedom of
$\sigma$ gives $\mu(\sigma(c_j)) \cap \mu(\sigma(c_i)) = \emptyset$. A union of
empty sets is empty, so the guard passes and the recursive call is made with
$W_{i-1} \cup \mu(s) = W_i$ and assignment $\sigma_i$. This proves the claim.

Taking $i = d$ in the claim, the call
$\mathrm{Extend}(d + 1, W_d, \sigma_d)$ occurs. Its first guard does not fire
because $k = \infty$, and its second guard $i > d$ does fire, so
$\sigma_d = \sigma$ is added to $R$.

Since $\sigma$ was an arbitrary conflict-free selection, every conflict-free
selection is reported, and if the instance is satisfiable at least one exists.
$\blacksquare$

> **Corollary 2 (termination).** $\mathrm{Solve}$ terminates on every finite
> instance.

*Proof.* Each recursive call strictly increases $i$, and no call is made once
$i > d$. The recursion depth is therefore at most $d + 1$. At each depth the
loop is finite, over $b_{c_i}$ sections. The recursion tree is finite, so the
procedure terminates. $\blacksquare$

### The cutoffs break completeness by design

Theorem 5 assumes no bound on results and no resource cutoff. The deployed
engine in [`app/src/core/scheduler.ts`](../app/src/core/scheduler.ts) imposes
four, and each one invalidates the theorem in a different way.

| Cutoff | Default | Effect on Theorem 5 |
|---|---|---|
| Result bound $k$ | 200 | The search stops early. Selections beyond the first $k$ are not reported. |
| Wall-clock deadline | 1200 ms | The search may abandon unexplored subtrees. |
| Node cap | $10^{6}$ | As above, on a different trigger. |
| State-space estimate cap | $5 \times 10^{6}$ | The search is refused before it begins, returning an empty result set. |

Under any of these, a satisfiable instance may produce no output. That is a
deliberate engineering choice for a browser session, where an unbounded search
would freeze the interface, and it is not a defect. It does mean that an empty
result from the deployed engine is not a proof of unsatisfiability. The last row
is the most consequential, because it returns an empty set without performing
any search at all, and an empty set is otherwise indistinguishable from a
genuine proof that no schedule exists.

Every experiment in [Section 6](./06-evaluation.md) disables all four cutoffs
explicitly, since a truncated traversal would report a partial node count as if
it were a complete one. The `UNBOUNDED` options in
[`app/src/bench/experiments.ts`](../app/src/bench/experiments.ts) do this, and
[Experiment 1](./06-evaluation.md#experiment-1) additionally asserts that no run
reached the result bound.

## 4.5 Complexity of the search

### Node count

The recursion tree has one level per course. Level $i$ contains one node per
partial assignment of the first $i$ courses, so its width is at most
$\prod_{j \le i} b_{c_j}$. The engine counts one node per section considered,
which is one per node of the tree below the root. Hence the total number of
nodes visited is at most

$$N_{\max} \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{d} \prod_{j \le i} b_{c_j}
\;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{d} \prod_{j \le i} b_{c_j} .$$

With a uniform branching factor $b_{c} = b$ this is a geometric sum,

$$N_{\max} \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{d} b^{i} \;=\; \frac{b^{d+1} - b}{b - 1} \;=\; O(b^{d}) .$$

Two remarks matter for reading [Section 6](./06-evaluation.md).

First, $N_{\max}$ is **not** $\prod_c b_c$. The product counts only the leaves,
that is, the complete assignments, while the node counter also counts internal
nodes. For $b = 4$ and $d = 2$ the product is $16$ while $N_{\max}$ is $20$, so
comparing a node count against the product can produce a fraction above one and
is simply the wrong bound. Both quantities are reported separately in the
evaluation for this reason.

Second, the bound is attained only when no pruning occurs, which happens exactly
when no candidate is ever rejected. Any rejection at depth $i$ removes the
entire subtree beneath it, of size $\sum_{m=i+1}^{d} \prod_{i < j \le m} b_{c_j}$.

### Cost per node

At each node the engine evaluates $W \cap \mu(s) = \emptyset$. Under the
factorisation $T = D \times P$ of
[Section 1.4](./01-preliminaries.md#14-the-deployed-encoding), and iterating
only the days on which $s$ actually meets, this costs $m(s)$ machine-word
conjunctions where $m(s) \le |D|$ is the number of meeting days. For the general
encoding with an unfactored universe it costs $\lceil |T| / w \rceil$
conjunctions.

The conflict test is therefore $\Theta(|T| / w)$ in general and $O(|D|)$ under
the deployed factorisation. It is $O(1)$ only when $|T|$ is fixed, which is the
case for the deployed configuration and not the case for the problem in general.
The unqualified claim that the check is $O(1)$, made by the earlier
documentation, holds only under that precondition.
[Experiment 3](./06-evaluation.md#experiment-3) measures both regimes.

### Total cost

Combining, the worst-case running time is

$$O\!\left( \frac{b^{d} \cdot |T|}{w} \right)
\qquad \text{and, for the deployed encoding,} \qquad
O\!\left( b^{d} \cdot |D| \right) .$$

The exponential factor is inherent given Theorem 3, unless $\mathrm{P} = \mathrm{NP}$.
The bitmask encoding acts entirely on the polynomial factor.

### Space

The recursion depth is $O(d)$ by Corollary 2. The pseudocode passes $W$ and $A$
by value, and the implementation does likewise, allocating a fresh week mask and
a fresh assignment at each placement. The live memory along a root-to-leaf path
is therefore
$$\sum_{i=1}^{d} \big( O(|D|) + O(i) \big) \;=\; O\!\left( d \cdot |D| + d^{2} \right) .$$
An implementation that mutated $W$ in place and undid the change on backtracking
would reduce this to $O(d + |D|)$. The engine does not do so, and
[Section 5.2](./05-implementation.md#52-allocation-behaviour) explains what this
means for the claim that the traversal is allocation-free.

## 4.6 The invariant made visible

**Figure 4.1** traces $\mathrm{Extend}$ on a three-course instance. All sections
meet on Monday only, so a mask is a single 12-bit integer and the invariant can
be checked by eye at every node. Bit $i$ corresponds to period $i$.

| Course | Section | Periods | $\beta(\mu(s))$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | $A_1$ | 0, 1 | 3 |
| A | $A_2$ | 2, 3 | 12 |
| B | $B_1$ | 1, 2 | 6 |
| B | $B_2$ | 4, 5 | 48 |
| C | $C_1$ | 0 | 1 |
| C | $C_2$ | 3 | 8 |

```
root                                  W = 0
|
+-- A1  (3)   0 AND 3 = 0    accept   W = 3
|   |
|   +-- B1  (6)    3 AND 6  = 2   reject, prune subtree
|   +-- B2  (48)   3 AND 48 = 0   accept   W = 51
|       |
|       +-- C1  (1)   51 AND 1 = 1   reject, prune
|       +-- C2  (8)   51 AND 8 = 0   accept   W = 59   SOLUTION {A1,B2,C2}
|
+-- A2  (12)  0 AND 12 = 0   accept   W = 12
    |
    +-- B1  (6)    12 AND 6  = 4   reject, prune
    +-- B2  (48)   12 AND 48 = 0   accept   W = 60
        |
        +-- C1  (1)   60 AND 1 = 0   accept   W = 61   SOLUTION {A2,B2,C1}
        +-- C2  (8)   60 AND 8 = 8   reject, prune
```

Ten nodes are visited and four are rejected. Reading the invariant at the node
$\{A_1, B_2\}$, condition 2 requires $W = \mu(A_1) \cup \mu(B_2)$, and indeed
$3 \vee 48 = 51$. Condition 3 requires $\mu(A_1) \cap \mu(B_2) = \emptyset$, and
indeed $3 \wedge 48 = 0$. The same reading applies at every accepted node in the
trace, which is what Lemma 4 asserts in general.

The unpruned tree for this instance would contain
$2 + 4 + 8 = 14$ nodes, so pruning saved four of the fourteen. At this size the
saving is small, which is the same effect
[Experiment 2](./06-evaluation.md#experiment-2) measures at the low end of its
range, where pruning is a net cost.
